Every enterprise AI initiative eventually hits the same wall. The infrastructure is in place. The data pipelines run. The models are fine-tuned. And yet the outputs are mediocre — technically correct, contextually wrong. The people who know what good looks like can spot the problem in seconds. They just cannot explain it in a way the system can use.

This is the tacit knowledge gap. It is not a technology problem. It is an epistemological one.

Tacit knowledge is the judgment that senior practitioners carry but rarely articulate. It is the account director who knows a brief is off before she can say why. The crisis manager who reads a situation differently from everyone else in the room. The strategist who rejects a direction that the data supports. This knowledge is valuable precisely because it is hard to transfer — it was built through years of pattern recognition, failure, and correction that no document captures.

Enterprise AI systems are trained on what organizations have written down. But what organizations write down is a sanitized, simplified version of how their best people actually think. The gap between those two things is where AI quality fails.

This report documents the problem and the solution. We drew on our work codifying expertise for senior practitioners across industries — marketing, crisis communications, market research, compliance — to identify the patterns that determine whether an AI system captures genuine expertise or merely the appearance of it. The findings are consistent enough to constitute a methodology.

The organizations that close the tacit knowledge gap do not do it by writing better documentation. They do it by building structured interview processes that surface judgment, and by creating feedback systems that let the expert correct the AI over time. The result is a system that improves with use rather than degrading into generic output.
